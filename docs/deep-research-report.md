# Claims Evaluation

1. **AGREE (major):** The *Self-evolving Network Verifiers* paper (arXiv:2608.11340, Aug 2026) indeed exists and uses a symbolic verifier with Batfish and containerlab FRR oracles.  This directly undermines the claim of being “first to compare” a model-based verifier against an emulator.  We found no prior work explicitly measuring \(P(\text{harm} \mid \text{verifier PASS})\) or a similar false-pass rate; literature search on that specific metric was inconclusive.  However, since the apparatus is already published, the novelty claim is dead and must be revised.  

2. **DISAGREE (major):** The surviving novelty claims are thin.  Claim (a) – quantifying \(P(\text{harm}|\text{PASS})\) – may be new, but only if no one else did it (we found none).  Claim (b) – spec-completeness sweep – is an ad-hoc fix for a known issue and not a standalone major insight.  Claim (c) – applying conformal risk control in networks – appears novel (no precedent found), but it’s essentially applying a known ICLR’24 method to a new domain, which is a moderate contribution.  Claim (d) – an “autonomy–risk curve” – is vague and could be seen as a simple analysis, not enough on its own for a full paper.  Overall, the novelty is questionable and likely too weak to carry the paper without stronger results.

3. **DISAGREE (major):** The “spec-completeness sweep” is not a statistically sound solution to the reviewer’s objection.  Using all subsets of properties (nested and non-independent) violates exchangeability and inflates type-I error.  Reusing the same candidates across nested subsets means dependence and multiple comparisons must be addressed (e.g. with Bonferroni or permutation tests), but the plan gives no approach.  Thus the sweep will yield biased error rates and cannot conclusively separate “recoverable” versus “irreducible” risk.  A more rigorous design (e.g. cross-validation with independent test sets, or controlling for multiplicity) is needed. 

4. **AGREE (minor):** The sample-size arithmetic is correct: for a 95% CI of ±5% at \(p\approx0.1\), one indeed needs ~138 samples per bin (Clopper–Pearson gives ~138.3), so 10 bins ≈ 1,380, hence ~6,900 PASS cases if only ~20% of candidates PASS.  Reducing to 4 equal-frequency bins (about 138×4≈552 PASS cases) is a reasonable compromise.  Alternatively, one could use continuous calibration curves with bootstrap confidence bands or Beta calibration (see below) instead of fixed bins.  The conclusion that 4 bins is more feasible is fair, but the plan should also mention methods like bootstrapping the reliability curve or using confidence intervals (e.g. Wilson intervals) for each bin.

5. **AGREE (minor):** Platt scaling (logistic) is more robust than isotonic when data is scarce; isotonic can indeed overfit with ≲1,000 points.  Beta calibration is a superior parametric alternative that flexibly fits skewed score distributions and contains the identity mapping, so it should be considered here.  Temperature scaling (a single-parameter logistic on logits) is similar to Platt.  The proposed nested grouped CV for full out-of-fold calibration is dubious: pooling predictions from different calibrators effectively mixes distributions and can bias the calibration (this is akin to cross-conformal prediction, which requires careful theory).  A safer approach would be a single held-out calibration set or a conformalized CV procedure.  The plan’s approach risks optimism in calibration and should be justified or replaced with established cross-validation calibration techniques.

6. **DISAGREE (major):** Calibrating at the incident level (one score per cluster) is not the correct fix.  It breaks candidate-level exchangeability but also ignores within-incident variation.  The correct approach is to use a conformal method for grouped or hierarchical data.  For example, Dunn et al. (JASA ’22/’23) develop *distribution-free prediction sets* for two-layer hierarchical data, and Principato et al. (arXiv ’24) introduce conformal procedures for hierarchical data.  These “grouped” or “hierarchical” conformal methods account for clustering while providing valid coverage.  The claim about α: with ~20 incidents, the smallest nonzero α one can guarantee is 1/(20+1)≈4.8%, so one cannot assert coverage at α≤0.05 (the statement is essentially correct).  But rather than simply rounding up α, the plan should cite these hierarchical conformal methods and explain how to apply them (e.g. repeated subsampling or group-conditional calibration).

7. **AGREE (minor):** Given only ~700 training samples, a deep CNN on adjacency “images” or a full GNN is likely to severely overfit.  The rejection of CNNs (due to no node ordering and ~10^4–10^6 parameters) and of a full GNN is reasonable at this sample size.  Gradient-boosted trees on tabular graph features is a safer choice.  A GNN *could* work only if there were thousands more examples or if one used transfer learning/pretraining on synthetic data, but that is not in the plan.  So the architecture choice is sensible: the hand-crafted graph features (betweenness, shortest paths etc.) provide strong inductive bias for limited data, and the small GNN ablation is fine as a sanity check.

8. **AGREE (minor):** More topologies with fewer incidents each likely increases generality (more structural diversity) without obvious downside, since total incidents is fixed.  One caveat is fewer incidents per topology means less precision in per-topology statistics (higher variance per topology).  In statistical terms, this increases heterogeneity across topologies and might require treating topology as a random effect.  However, the overall sample size stays the same, and doubling the number of topologies does not reduce the total information (and improves cross-validation folds).  So the trade-off seems acceptable, with only minor impact on variance.

9. **AGREE (minor):** Using 150 Batfish-FAIL cases to bound the false-alarm rate via the “rule of three” is sensible.  The rule-of-three (95% upper bound ≈3/n) says 0/150 → ≤2%, matching the binomial bound of about 1.95%.  Thus 150 is a reasonable choice to get a ~2% upper bound on the false alarm rate.  One could compute the exact Clopper–Pearson interval instead of the rule-of-three, but the approximation is fine here.  The assumption is that these 150 cases are iid draws from the “Batfish FAIL” population; if not, the bound could be off, but as given the approach is acceptable.

10. **DISAGREE (major):** Mapping NO_CONVERGENCE to harmful=1 is conservative (treating any non-convergent routing as failure is reasonable).  However, **excluding** UNDETERMINED and EXECUTION_FAILED cases likely biases the harm estimate downward.  If, for example, non-labeled cases occur more often when the change is risky (e.g. causing simulator issues), ignoring them underestimates the true \(P(\text{harm}|\text{PASS})\).  The plan’s current code silently treating all three as safe=0 is also wrong.  In summary, the treatment should be: count NO_CONVERGENCE as harmful (1), but either impute or analyze UNDETERMINED/FAILED separately – dropping them without adjustment will make the config repair method appear safer than it really is.

11. **DISAGREE (major):** Concluding that “risk is not predictable” and settling for bounds sounds weak for a NOMS/CNSM/TNSM paper.  Negative results can sometimes be publishable if very surprising, but here the only positive insight is “fit no model works, so use conformal bounds,” which is not a strong result by itself.  At best this might be a workshop or short paper.  Top venues expect either a novel algorithm or an insightful theory or surprising empirical finding; merely showing that learned models fail (and a trivial rule works) is unlikely to pass.  Unless the authors can frame a more general principle (e.g. fundamental unpredictability of this risk), this outcome risks rejection for lack of contribution.

# Further Analyses

**A. Top 3 Likely Errors (ranked):** (1) *Novelty overstated.* The core claim of novelty is undermined by Protogeros et al. (2026) and possibly others, so the paper’s main contribution vanishes.  (2) *Statistical/methodological flaws.* The spec-sweep lacks proper statistical control, and the conformal calibration ignores cluster structure, making key results invalid.  (3) *Premature negative result.* The plan expects no successful model, which undermines publishable outcomes; relying on a negative result and trivial rule is a risky bet for these venues.

**B. Missing Considerations:** The plan overlooks key confounds: **synthetic bias** (all repairs are from chosen parametric operators, not real human fixes), **topology bias** (all ten test topologies come from one generator, limiting generality), and **simulation variability** (only one emulator run per candidate – no consideration of stochasticity or multiple runs).  It also doesn’t consider using or mining complete specifications (e.g. Config2Spec) as an alternative baseline, nor the noise in FRR vs hardware behavior.

**C. Likely Review Verdict:** A strong rejection reason is *lack of significant novelty combined with flawed evaluation*.  Even if the experiments are executed, the contributions (risk quantification + conformal) are too incremental, and key claims have counterexamples in literature.  The plan fails to convincingly address the “first” claims.  In this scenario, a reviewer would likely say “interesting effort but not enough substance.”  The plan as written cannot survive that: it must incorporate missing rigorous methods and clarify novelty.  Without that, the paper would be viewed as a near-trivial empirical study with limited insight.
